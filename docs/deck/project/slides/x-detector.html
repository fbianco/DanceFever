The whole frame, letterboxed to 224 × 224, goes through a convolutional network that scores 2,254 anchors: fixed reference boxes on 28 × 28, 14 × 14 and 7 × 7 grids (28²·2 + 14²·2 + 7²·6).

Each anchor regresses 12 numbers (a box and 4 keypoints) and one logit z, scored σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^−z). Overlapping hits are merged by weighted non-maximum suppression.

The keypoints give the alignment (BlazePose): hip centre c, a radius r enclosing the body, and the tilt θ of the hip-to-shoulder line.

p_img = c + s·R(θ)·(q − ½), q ∈ [0,1]², s ∝ r

R(θ) = [cos θ, −sin θ ; sin θ, cos θ]

The crop is rotated so the dancer is always upright and centred: the landmark network never has to learn tilted bodies.

The detector is a single-shot detector in the SSD family. The frame is letterboxed to 224 by 224 and a convolutional network produces, for each of 2,254 anchors, 12 regression outputs and one classification logit; the shapes [1, 2254, 12] and [1, 2254, 1] are read from the model file. The count matches two anchors per cell on a 28 by 28 and a 14 by 14 feature map, plus six per cell on 7 by 7. The 12 numbers are a box and four keypoints. Following the BlazePose paper by Bazarevsky and colleagues, those keypoints encode an alignment: the midpoint of the hips, a circle radius enclosing the whole person, and the incline of the line from mid-hip to mid-shoulder. That gives a similarity transform from the unit square of the crop to the image. Everything downstream works in that normalised, upright frame, which is a big reason why a 1.4 million weight network is enough.
